Inheriting Property in Taiwan: A Guide for Overseas Heirs (2026)

Liubai Studio · Updated 2026-07

If you live in the United States, Canada, Japan, Australia, or Singapore and your parents own real property in Taiwan, the inheritance process runs on Taiwan-side deadlines that start on the date of death — whether or not you are ready, and whether or not you can travel. Missing them means penalties, late-filing fines, and in the worst case a forced path through court procedures. This guide walks through the Taiwan-side rules as they stand in 2026: what must be filed, when, what documents an overseas heir needs, and a tax trap that catches non-resident heirs when the property is eventually sold.

Deadline 1: Estate tax must be filed within 6 months of death

Taiwan's estate tax return is due within 6 months of the date of death, with one possible extension of 3 months on application. The clock does not pause because heirs live abroad.

Key 2026 figures for the calculation:

  • Basic exemption: NT$13.33 million.
  • Deductions: surviving spouse NT$5.53 million; each lineal descendant NT$560,000; each surviving parent NT$1.38 million; funeral expense deduction NT$1.38 million; special deduction for a severely disabled heir NT$6.93 million.
  • Two-year clawback: property gifted to heirs within 2 years before death is added back into the taxable estate.

One deduction overseas families often overlook: under Civil Code Article 1030-1, a surviving spouse may claim half of the difference in post-marriage property between the spouses, and that amount is deducted from the gross estate before tax. For estates where one spouse held most of the assets, this can materially reduce the bill. Actual tax is determined by Taiwan's tax authority.

Deadline 2: Inheritance registration within 6 months

Separately from the tax filing, Land Act Article 73 requires heirs to apply for inheritance registration of real property within 6 months from the commencement of inheritance. Late applications are subject to escalating fines. In practice the sequence is: file the estate tax return, obtain the tax clearance certificate, then register the transfer at the land office. In Taiwan, this registration is performed by licensed land administration agents (地政士), and tax filings are prepared by licensed CPAs or tax agents — these are regulated professions, and any coordinator working for you should be dispatching them, not replacing them.

Two related deadlines sit alongside these:

  • Renouncing an inheritance (for example, where debts exceed assets) must be done in writing to the court within 3 months of learning you are an heir.
  • Heirs residing in mainland China are subject to a separate rule: a written declaration of intent to inherit must be filed within 3 years. If your family includes mainland-resident heirs, flag this early with your Taiwan-side professionals.

Documents an overseas heir needs

The paperwork is where most overseas families lose weeks. A typical package includes:

  1. Death certificate — if the parent passed away abroad, the foreign death certificate must be authenticated and translated for use in Taiwan.
  2. Household registration records proving the family relationship, plus a table of heirs (繼承系統表).
  3. Identity documents for each heir; heirs without current Taiwan household registration use passports and authenticated equivalents.
  4. Power of attorney — the critical item. An heir who cannot travel signs an authorization letter appointing a representative in Taiwan, and that signature must be authenticated at a Taiwan overseas mission (a Taipei Economic and Cultural Office or equivalent representative office) in your country of residence. An unauthenticated POA will be rejected by land offices and banks.
  5. Estate inventory — property registration transcripts, bank balances, and any farmland agricultural-use certificates.

Because authentication appointments at overseas missions can take weeks to obtain, this step should start almost immediately after the death, not after the family has finished discussing how to divide the estate.

The non-resident capital-gains trap: which heir sells matters

Inheriting the property is only half the story. When the property is later sold, Taiwan's consolidated housing and land income tax ("House-Land 2.0") applies, and the rules treat resident and non-resident sellers very differently.

For Taiwan tax residents, rates fall with holding period: 45% if held under 2 years, 35% for 2–5 years, 20% for 5–10 years, and 15% beyond 10 years. Two features are heir-friendly: the decedent's holding period counts toward yours, and the cost basis for inherited property is the assessed house value plus announced land value at the time of inheritance. A resident seller who has maintained household registration and self-use for 6 years may also claim the self-use exemption: the first NT$4 million of gain tax-free, and 10% on the excess.

For non-residents — which typically includes heirs living full-time in the US, Canada, Japan, Australia, or Singapore — the rates are a flat 45% (held under 2 years) or 35% (2 years or more), with no self-use exemption and no repurchase refund, regardless of how long the family held the property.

Illustrative example

This is an illustrative example only. A mother bought a Taipei apartment in 2000 and passes away in 2026, leaving it to two children: one living in Taiwan, one in California. Basis at inheritance (assessed house value plus announced land value) is NT$9 million. The apartment sells in 2027 for NT$15 million. After deducting the land value increment amount of NT$1 million already taxed under land value increment tax, taxable gain is NT$5 million. Combined holding period exceeds 10 years.

  • Taiwan-resident heir sells: 15% rate → tax of NT$750,000. If the self-use conditions were met, NT$4 million would be exempt and only NT$100,000 of tax would apply.
  • Non-resident heir sells: flat 35% → tax of NT$1.75 million.

Same apartment, same price — a difference of NT$1 million or more purely on which heir holds title at the time of sale. This is why estate division agreements among overseas siblings deserve tax modeling before anyone signs.

On the same sale, land value increment tax (LVIT) also applies at progressive rates of 20/30/40%, with a 10% once-in-a-lifetime self-use rate (up to 300 m² urban / 700 m² non-urban), long-term holding reductions of 20/30/40% for land held over 20/30/40 years, and a repurchase refund for a replacement self-use home bought within 2 years (which must not change use within 5 years). Farmland is its own category

Agricultural land that is genuinely in agricultural use, with an agricultural-use certificate, transfers without LVIT and passes by inheritance or gift free of estate and gift tax — but it is then monitored for 5 years, and if agricultural use stops during that period the exempted tax is clawed back. Overseas heirs who cannot personally farm the land need a plan before claiming the exemption.

Frequently asked questions

Do I have to fly to Taiwan to inherit my parents' property?

Usually no. Most steps can be completed through a representative in Taiwan acting under a power of attorney, provided your signature is authenticated at a Taiwan overseas mission in your country. Some banks apply their own stricter rules for releasing deposits, so account matters occasionally require a visit — real property registration generally does not.

What if we miss the 6-month deadlines?

A late estate tax filing exposes the estate to penalties on top of the tax, and late inheritance registration under Land Act Article 73 triggers escalating fines. If the 6-month tax deadline is approaching, apply for the 3-month extension before it lapses — the extension must be requested, not assumed.

Will I also owe tax or reporting in my own country?

Possibly. Receiving a foreign inheritance may carry tax or reporting obligations in your country of residence — the US, Canada, Japan, Australia, and Singapore each treat it differently. Those rules are outside the scope of Taiwan-side coordination, and you should consult a licensed tax professional in your country of residence. This article addresses Taiwan-side obligations only.
